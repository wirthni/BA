\section{Quark gluon plasma physics at colliders}
\subsection{The QGP as a Quantum Chromodynamics Object}
Quantum Chromodynamics, or QCD, is the quantum field theory describing strong interactions, obeying the experimental observation of asymptotic freedom as a key ingredient. \cite{peskin_introduction_2018} In 1963, Gell-Mann and Zweig have introduced quarks as elementary constituents of composite particles in need for a model that is able to explain the spectrum of strongly interacting particles. In this model, mesons are expected to be made up of quark-antiquark bound states while baryons are interpreted as bound states of three quarks, each chosen from a tuple of three possible quarks, up(u), down(d) and strange(s) which is the minimum number of different quarks to explain already known quantum numbers and charges of hadrons. Three other quark species(flavors) have followed, namely charm(c), bottom(b) and top(t) in order to explain the discovery of additional hadrons. The charges of the quarks are fractional, they are $+2/3$ for (u,c,t) and $-1/3$ for (d,s,b). If we for now only consider transformations among the light quark triplet (u,d,s), it has been found that elementary particles naturally fill out irreducible representations of a $SU(3)_F$ symmetry. Nevertheless, this quark model alone does not explain certain observations, like that one never encounters free particles with a fractional charge in nature or that there exists a quark $\Delta^{++}$(uuu) with spin $3/2$ that has a fully symmetric wave function despite made up of quarks, that are fermions and constructed to obey Fermi-Dirac statistics. The solution to this problem required the introduction of another quantum number for quarks ,the color, in which baryon wave functions must be antisymmetric in. It comes out there are three possible color charges for quarks ($i = 1,2,3$), making them transform under the fundamental representation '$3$' of the $SU(3)_{C(olor)}$ symmetry while antiquarks transform in the '$\bar{3}$' representation. Under the postulate theat all hadron wave functions are invariant under $SU(3)$ transformations, there are only little combinations allowed

\begin{equation}
	\bar{q}^i q_i \quad \epsilon^{ijk} q_i q_j q_k \quad \epsilon_{ijk} \bar{q}^i \bar{q}^j \bar{q}^k .
\end{equation}

which are the wave functions of known light baryons and mesons. \cite{peskin_introduction_2018} If one tries to separate a color-singlet state into colored components, for example by dissociating a meson into a quark and an antiquark, gluons, the quanta of the $SU(3)$ gauge field, form between the quark-antiquark pair making the energy cost to separate them grow proportionally to the distance. This phenomenon is known as confinement of color and explains why no free quarks can be observed. The other case, in which quarks are confined to baryons and mesons, refers to the asymptotically free state where they only interact weakly and can be described as almost-free particles. 

In contrast to the description above it has been known since the 1970s that strongly compressed matter, like present in the very early moments of our universe or in neutron stars, cannot be composed of individual hadrons. \cite{collins_superdense_1975} To understand the reasoning behind this claim one needs to remember that a neutron has a density of about $8 \cdot 10^{14} \frac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^3}$, but the central density of a neutron star is ten to 100 times larger. Meaning that the hadrons inside of a neutron star must overlap, losing their individuality and making up a state of matter that may casually be called a quark soup, formally referred to as quark gluon plasma (QGP). This form of matter that made up the very early universe must have had temperatures above above the fundamental energy scale of quantum chromodynamics, which can be first-order approximated as the inverse of the size of a hadron that underlies QCD. \cite{busza_heavy_2018} Taking into account that heavy ion collisions recreate droplets of this hot matter and therefore play a major role in the understanding of what has happened in the very early moments of the universe is a powerful motivation for studying ultra-relativistic heavy ion collisions.

\subsection{Phenomenologies of a heavy ion collision}

Let us now look at the current understanding of a heavy ion nuclei collision taking into account the already introduced model assumptions and observed phenomenologies to gain an understanding of how they help us understand QCD. With the assumption that incident nuclei are sphere-like objects, they appear as Lorentz contracted discs in the lab frame as they move towards each other at speeds close to the speed of light. \cite{busza_heavy_2018} The thickness of the disc depends on the order of length contraction and therefore on the gamma factor $\gamma$, its diameter is invariant under this Lorentz transformation. Each disk contains quarks, antiquarks and $q\bar{q}$ pairs due to quantum fluctuations that are decelerated in time due to the time dilation effect, making them noticeable. The strong interaction between the discs can be described in terms of interacting fields , because as the two discs collide, some color charge exchange occurs longitudinally between the discs, producing longitudinal color fields. These color fields can then gradually decay into $q\bar{q}$ pairs and gluons. In heavy ion collisions, the maximum energy density occurs just as the two highly Lorentz contracted nuclei collide  \cite{busza_heavy_2018} and to qualitatively describe the state of the nuclei shortly after the collision, we must know what volume energy density dominates at a specific time, for example $1\unit{fm/c}$ after the collision when the discs are separated by $2\unit{fm}$. Lattice QCD calculations of QCD thermodynamics show that matter in thermal equilibrium under these circumstances has an energy density of about $12.7\unit{GeV/fm}^3$ which exceeds the typical energy density inside of a hadron ($\approx 500\unit{MeV/fm}^3$) by a factor of around 20, meaning that partons under these circumstances cannot be described as individual hadrons. Instead, under their strong coupling they behave like a relativistic hydrodynamic fluid with a remarkably low viscosity, the quark gluon plasma.
As the QGP behaves liquid-like and the pressure is still high (remind the large energy density, which is equivalent to pressure), the pressure-driven hydrodynamic expansion rapidly builds up large transverse velocities although they were small initially. \cite{busza_heavy_2018} But the quark gluon plasma is not only produced at the very center of the collision due to the strong time dilation of the quarks and gluons inside of the discs and their fast propagation in beam direction. When enough time has passed in their frame of reference, much time has passed in the lab frame, meaning the QGP is also produced at larger distances, making the disc gradually lose energy as partons with higher rapidity separate from it. If we look at an arbitrary volume with a sufficiently high energy density, each volume unit of QGP expands in all directions, but only until a chosen space volume has an energy density below that of an individual hadron, which makes the fluid fall apart into a mist of individual hadrons. It is also interesting to take a closer look at the phase transition from the QGP to the low-temperature individual hadron phase. Emerging results from work in lattice QCD in the 2000s \cite{karsch_lattice_2002} \cite{aoki_order_2006} regarding calculations on pressure and energy density of hot QCD matter containing equal densities of quarks and antiquarks state that the transition from hot QCD matter to hadronic matter proceeds via a continuous crossover, not a first order phase transition. \cite{busza_heavy_2018}. This kind of particle production is very different from the particle production in elementary collisions where only a few new particles are created.
For the following chapters, it is important to give a quick notice on non-central collisions. When looking at two non-centrally colliding nuclei longitudinally, that is in beam direction, one should notice that the overlap region of the two discs has an initial approximately lenticular shape in the transverse plane, \cite{busza_heavy_2018} making the shape of the original QGP non-spherical. This results in anisotropies in the pressure of the hydrodynamic fluid, which in turn influences the expansion velocity and hence the azimuthal momentum distribution of the finally produced particles.

\subsection{Particle jets as a QGP probe}

Let uns now look at the collision of individual partons in the incident nuclei, to be precise at hard collisions between them. In such hard collisions it may happen that particles with large transverse momenta $\mathcal{O} (10\unit{GeV})$ are produced, which are very important for analyzing the properties of the QGP. \cite{busza_heavy_2018} They can be traced back to the earliest moments of the collision where high-energy parton pairs and electroweak bosons are produced. They will then evolve, decay, radiate and therefore produce a cone-shaped spray of hadrons and high-energy photons, leptons or heavy $Q\bar{Q}$ pairs, or a jet, all within the region where QGP is being produced and evolved. Therefore, the jet constituents contain some kind of information about the produced QGP medium, like an X-ray scan, having documented how partons have lost energy in the medium or, on the other hand, influence the medium. \newline
But one needs to exactly know how jets behave in a non-QGP environment to be able to extract information from their behavior in presence of the QGP. \cite{busza_heavy_2018} The production of hard, so high-$p_T$ $\gamma$ and $Z^0$ has been studied in pp and AA (heavy ion) collisions. \cite{collaboration_measurement_2013} \cite{collaboration_study_2015} \cite{collaboration_centrality_2016} Furthermore the measured production rates in AA heavy ion collisions are in excellent agreement with the production rate in pp collisions, multiplied with the number of nucleon interactions that happen during the overlap when the nuclei pass by. Note that this underlies small corrections, because the nucleons are inside of the nuclei. Since the QGP is a QCD object and $\gamma$ and $Z^0$ are colorless, they are not affected by the post-collision AA environment. On the other hand, strongly interacting, colored, hard probes like jets, high $p_T$ hadrons, heavy quarks and so on, can be used to compare AA to pp collisions in order to investigate how the colored probes themselves are modified by the QGP medium. Experimental measurements of jets , high-$p_T$ hadrons and heavy quarks coming from jets \cite{connors_review_2018} have shown that jets lose a noticeable amount of energy as they propagate through the QGP, with the energy ending up in as many soft particles moving at large angles relative to the jet axis. \cite{collaboration_measurement_2016} Another effect is that when particles travel at a speed faster than the velocity of sound in a medium, like an aircraft flying at supersonic speed, they generate Mach waves that can add up to a shockwave, called the Mach cone. \cite{sasoh_compressible_2020} Such a mach cone can also be produced inside the quark gluon plasma by propagating jets. \cite{casalderrey-solana_conical_2005} The study of such a QGP Mach cone can provide important information about the properties of the QGP, such as transport coefficients or the equation of state. \cite{yang_3d_2023} Nevertheless, this Mach cone has never been observed unambiguously, because when averaging the soft Mach cone particles over their possible propagation angles, their distribution becomes a nondescript Gaussian similar to that of soft hadrons from jet fragmentation. \cite{chen_effects_2018} \cite{chen_search_2021}

The collection of such phenomena is called "jet quenching" and makes up a very important part of QGP studies, as they give us direct evidence of strong interactions between the jet and the liquid. \cite{busza_heavy_2018}

\subsection{The diffusion wake}

Shock waves that form a Mach cone in the quark gluon plasma are always accompanied by a diffusion wake behind the propagating jet emerging from the hydrodynamic description of the medium response to the energy-momentum deposition. \cite{neufeld_mach_2009} \cite{ke_qgp_2021} The diffusion wake emerges, because the energy of jet-induced medium partons is negative behind the propagating jet parton, which depletes the thermal medium parton density behind the path of the propagating jet partons. \cite{he_linear_2015} A direct consequence of such diffusion wake is the depletion of soft hadrons in the final hadron spectra in the opposite direction to the propagating jet. \cite{chen_search_2021} No other medium effect competes this depletion signal.
