\section{Analysis of heavy ion collision data}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{Media/Pictures/EllipticFlow}
	\caption{Schematic illustrations of a $\sqrt{S_{NN}} = 200\unit{GeV}$ Au+Au collision with a 6 fm impact parameter. The left panel shows the nucleons of the two colliding nuclei with an ellipse outlining the approximate interaction region. The right panel shows a momentum-space representation of $v_2$. The average radius of each successive ring represents the $p_T$ of the particles while the anisotropy of the ring represents the magnitude of $v_2$. The highest $p_T$ particles (outer-ring) exhibit the strongest $v_2$ while the lowest $p_T$ particles (inner-ring) exhibit a vanishingly small $v_2$. From \cite{collaboration_centrality_2021}}
	\label{fig:ellipticflow}
\end{figure}

When analyzing heavy ion collision data from ALICE, one has to know first which quantities, may they be event-related or particle-, so track-related, are important to be taken care of for each specific analysis. For serving the purpose of this thesis, we will therefore only take into account those quantities that are or could be needed to conduct an heavy ion analysis regarding the diffusion wake. Starting at where all particles come from, let us first look at interesting event, so collision, properties. In the first chapter we have talked about the phenomenologies of when two heavy ion nuclei collide at collider energies in the case where they fully overlap. What has not been talked about yet is what happens when two nuclei collide only peripherally, or non-centrally. The centrality of the collision of two nuclei is characterized by the impact parameter $b$ between the nuclei, so the distance between their centers in the transverse direction. \cite{collaboration_centrality_2021} The smaller the impact parameter is, the bigger the overlap region of the nuclei is, thus increasing the size of the resulting medium. The outcome of the collision directly depends on the number of nucleons that participate, but this quantity is not directly accessible and hence needs to be derived from other quantities that scale approximately with the number of participating nucleons, such as the particle multiplicity. The centrality of events is categorized from 0-100\%, where low values describe the most central events, e.g. 0-10\%. An effect that emerges from the non-centrality of collisions is elliptic flow. When nuclei collide non-centrally, the overlap region is shaped like a lentil, shown in figure \ref{fig:ellipticflow}. Given this initial lentil shape of the overlap region, it is interesting to look at whether this shape also shows up in the distribution of particles in the momentum space.  \cite{collaboration_centrality_2021} A Fourier decomposition is used to describe the azimuthal dependence of the final momentum-space distributions: \cite{sorensen_elliptic_2010}

\begin{equation}
	\frac{d^3 N}{p_T d p_T dy d(\phi - \psi)} = \frac{1}{2 \pi} \frac{d N}{p_T d p_T dy} \cdot \left[1 + v_1 \cos(\phi - \psi) + 2 v_2 \cos(2 (\phi - \psi)) + \dots \right]
\end{equation}

$\phi$ is the azimuth angle of the particle, $y$ is the longitudinal rapidity, $p_T$ the transverse momentum an $\psi$ is the reaction plane angle defined by the vector connecting the centers of the two colliding nuclei. That is the $x$-axis in the case of figure \ref{fig:ellipticflow}. The reaction plane angle $\psi$ is not known and is estimated using the transverse distribution of particles in the final state. The acquired approximation is called event plane angle \cite{poskanzer_methods_1998}, but since the reaction plane angle is not known after all, we will call the event plane angle $\psi$ in the following. $v_2 > 0$ describes the case where more particles are emitted along the short axis of the overlap region. The bigger $v_2$, the stronger the particle correlation asymmetry is.  It comes out that $v_2 > 0$ for typical per-nucleon energies at the LHC, \cite{collaboration_centrality_2021} so we will expect an enhancement of particles in the direction of the reaction plane, that is how the event plane is defined, then. Because we will be investigating particle correlation differences at orders of magnitude smaller than the effect of the elliptic flow, $\psi$ will be an event property to keep in mind.

The position of the primary vertex $(x,y,z)$ describes how far the event has happened away form the nominal interaction point and will be needed for only selecting events that have a low $z$ value. We do this, because data taking is only calibrated for a certain range around the nominal interaction point. The multiplicity $N$ of an event will hold the number of charged particle tracks in the corresponding event. Since we will be taking particle correlation differences between different events, it may become important to do so between similarly populated events.

Particles, or tracks, will have individual properties, measured by a combination of the ALICE detector systems, including the particle charge $q$, the initial relativistic momentum $\vec{p} = (P^1, P^2, P^3) = (p_x, p_y, p_z)$, the particle dependent calorimeter energy loss $dE/dx$ for possible particle identification and the TPC track length. We will remove particles that have their reconstructed trajectories far away from the primary vertex position. This is characterized by the distance to closest approach (DCA) values DCA$_z$ and DCA$_{xy}$.

From these particle properties one can deduce further useful quantities. Since most of the particles created in Pb-Pb collisions at ALICE are pions \cite{ortiz_results_2014}, we will be assuming $m = m(\pi^0)$, resulting in $P^0 = E = \sqrt{m_\pi^2 + |\vec{p}|^2}$. According to the ALICE coordinate system (figure \ref{fig:alicecoordinates}), the azimuthal angle can be acquired by $\phi = \atan(p_y, p_x)$, the transversal momentum is $p_T = \sqrt{p_x^2 + p_y^2}$ and the pseudorapidity is defined as \cite{busza_heavy_2018} $\eta = -\ln(\tan(\frac{\theta}{2}))$. Most of the data analysis will take place in the $(\phi, \eta, p_T)$ phase space or a subset of it and to get an idea of how data will look like in this case, figure \ref{fig:dijet2dexample} shows an exemplary event three-dimensionally. The $(\phi, \eta)$ plane can be thought of as the unwrapped surface area of the barrel-shaped detector. The vertical axis indicates the particle's transversal momentum.
