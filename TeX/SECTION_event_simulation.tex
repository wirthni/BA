\section{Event simulation: The identification of potential biases}

\subsection{Motivation}

When conducting analyses in heavy ion physics or particle physics one usually has to select experimental data from a very huge data collection, for example recorded events from the Large Hadron Collider. However, this data selection process is nontrivial and has obvious, but also hidden consequences on the outcome of the analysis. To give a demonstration on that, let us consider a conceptionally simple measurement and two possible traps that lead to a wrong analysis result. As an example we would like to measure the average elliptic flow parameter $v_2$ in Pb--Pb collisions (see chapter \ref{sec:analysis-of-heavy-ion-collision-data} for an introduction into flow physics). For our analysis, we will be using a sample of Pb--Pb data in the centrality category of 0-10\%, that would be the most central collisions. After conducting the analysis, we get a certain value for $v_2$. But this value is for sure not representative for Pb--Pb collisions in general, because the cut on very central events has removed all peripheral events from the analysis. Therefore, the value of $v_2$ in peripheral events is omitted in the analysis. We call this a selection bias. Another error that could happen in this analysis is to also take high-$p_T$ particles into account in the particle correlation from which the $v_2$ flow parameter is derived. This is not correct, because high-$p_T$ particles, for example as constituents of jets, are not subject to flow. One can see that such mistakes, may they be obvious or worth a closer look, can be made in many stages of the analysis. If we want to make sure that we have not done any mistakes of such kind, we can use a simulation of the real data to crosscheck the analysis result. Back at the diffusion wake measurement we can therefore conclude, that we should not see any diffusion wake signal when conducted with simulated data if we have not implemented any physics of hydrodynamics, which is the origin of the diffusion wake. On the other hand, if the simulated data analysis shows signals of the same kind and order of magnitude as the diffusion wake, it is possible to have missed out an elementary mistake in the analysis method.

\subsection{Modeling the simulation}

Each simulated Pb--Pb event will be generated as a Monte Carlo generated overlay of thermal background from the medium expansion and hadronization, and artificially generated jets from hard scatterings. Both components do not interfere with each other, meaning that the jets are not subject to jet quenching and the medium is not affected by the artificially inserted jets.

The thermal background will involve elliptic and triangular ($v_2, v_3 \neq 0$) flow for each particle. The background is generated based on statistics of $0-10\%$ ALICE Pb--Pb data from the RUN 2 period at $\sqrt{s_{NN}} = 5.02\bunit{TeV}$ \cite{nadine_alice_grunwald_studies_2022} and also $0-5\%$ ALICE Pb--Pb data at $\sqrt{s_{NN}} = 2.76\bunit{TeV}$ \cite{collaboration_pseudorapidity_2016}. The utilized 0--10\% statistics involve information on charged hadron multiplicity probability densities $p(N)$, transverse momentum probability densities $p(p_T)$ and also track transverse momentum dependent elliptic flow parameter statistics $v_2(p_T)$. Because the $v_2(p_T)$ statistics also include higher-$p_T$ particles that are not subject to flow, it has been corrected such that $\lim_{p_T \rightarrow \infty}  v_2(p_T) = 0$ empirically by fitting an exponentially decaying function to the data points below $p_T \leq 5\unit{GeV}$. This value has been chosen, because the logarithmic representation of the particle $p_T$ spectrum of Pb--Pb collisions shows an exponential decay according to Boltzmann statistics for $p_T \in [0, 5]\unit{GeV}$ (see figure \ref{fig:simulationinput}). Such low transverse momenta are therefore dominated by thermal particles that are subject to flow. For higher values, this exponential decay gets distorted by other effects like particles from hard parton interactions and jets. Information on the decreasing value of $v_2$ with an increasing $|\eta|$ is provided too coarse for mid-rapidity analyses. \cite[Figure 1, $v_2\{2\}$ for 0--5\%,][]{collaboration_pseudorapidity_2016} That is why it has been modeled with linear fit empirically for data points that fulfill $|\eta| \leq 1.25$. Since $v_2(\eta)$ is only known globally for particles with a transverse momentum below $5\bunit{GeV}$, it is understood as a reduction factor. In practice the particle's individual $v_2(p_T)$ will be reduced by the fraction that $v_2(\eta)$ decreases by for the particle's pseudorapidity $\eta$ relative to $\eta = 0$. A collection of the statistics that were used to simulate the thermal background are shown in figure \ref{fig:simulationinput}.

\begin{equation}
	\begin{gathered}
		v_2(p_T) = a \cdot (p_T)^b \cdot \exp(-c \cdot p_T)\\
		v_2(\eta) = d - e \cdot |\eta| \\
		\text{with constants} \\
		a=6.3 \cdot 10^{-2}\\
		b=1.6 \cdot 10^{0}\\
		c=4.7\cdot 10^{-1} \\
		d=2.6\cdot 10^{-2}\\
		e=3.9\cdot 10^{-3} \, .
	\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

The astute reader may have noticed that the background's transverse momentum probability density will produce high-$p_T$ particles that are not considered background. Due to the indifferentiability of background particles and non-background particles in real data, it is difficult to produce a transverse momentum statistics that only involves background particles. Nevertheless, because the probability density decreases exponentially, low-$p_T$ particles will strongly dominate the particle spectrum, making the occasionally produced high-$p_T$ particles negligible.

To fully simulate Pb--Pb events, realistically distributed high-$p_T$ jets are still missing. This data is generated using the PYTHIA \cite event generator.

The recipe for generating a Pb--Pb event from scratch is pretty straightforward given the introduced data. The first step is to add all $N_\text{PYTHIA}$ PYTHIA particles to the event. Then, a particle multiplicity $N$ is generated based on the probability distribution $p(N)$ from which the number of PYTHIA particles is subtracted, so $N_\text{Background} = N - N_\text{PYTHIA}$. As other event-wise random parameters, the event plane angle $\psi_2$ as well as the triangular flow offset angle $\psi_3$ is chosen in the interval $\psi_{2,3} \in [-\pi/2, +\pi/2]$. Each of the $N_\text{Background}$ particles is then generated with the transverse momentum being determined first from $p(p_T)$. After that, a pseudorapidity is chosen randomly in the interval $\eta \in [-0.9, 0.9]$. Since now the transverse momentum as well as the pseudorapidity are known, it is possible to calculate $v_2(p_T, \eta)$ and also $v_3(p_T, \eta) \equiv v_2(p_T, \eta)$. This was chosen as a simplification, because the order of magnitudes of both values are the same for heavy ion particle multiplicities. \cite{alqahtani_understanding_2026} With the values of $v_2, v_3, \psi_2, \psi_3$ one can generate a probability density distribution of where the particle will be generated in azimuthal angle, that is 

\begin{equation}
	p(\phi) = 1 + 2 \left[v_2 \cos(2(\phi - \psi_2)) + v_3 \cos(3(\phi - \psi_3)) \right] \, .
\end{equation}

With that, a particle has now been assigned a tuple $(\phi, \eta, p_T, m)$ from which all other properties can be derived from. The particle-wise procedure is repeated $N_\text{Background}$ times. An exemplary generated event is shown in figure \ref{fig:generatedevent}. Since the background is generated randomly, one PYTHIA event is reused multiple times with other background samples.
